### AI Governance

I've been doing a lot of technical reading, so let's shift over to AI governance. It might be useful one day.

### A Little More Than US, But Less Than EU

When it comes to AI, the United Kingdom is "less cautious than the EU but more willing to address risks than the US." That quote is from the paper, "[From Turing to Tomorrow: The UK's Approach to AI Regulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03050)," by Oliver Ritchie, Markus Anderljung, and Tom Rachman. 

Brits have played an outsized role in AI development: Babbage, Lovelace, Turing, DeepMind, and Geoffrey Hinton, who I had assumed was Canadian.  Hinton led the team responsible for many AI breakthroughs at the University of Toronto. Around the same time, Deep Mind was started in London in 2010. 

After leaving the EU, the UK adopted a "wait-and-see" approach to AI. Throughout the 2010s, most of the focus on AI policy concerned social media algorithms. In 2023, UK convenes the first AI Safety Summit. 

The paper then goes through a litany of issues related to AI that British policymakers need to consider. Not surprisingly, these are identical to the issues confronting American legislators. Something the authors argue for is that there is no need to "create separate rules for AI systems," but rather update existing legal frameworks.  That recommendation is in the context of AI discrimination, but I think that approach is a sound one for many AI issues. The existing legal framework, at least in the US, shouldn't need to be revamped for AI. 

I feel like the authors missed some crucial data points.  Although they rightly note that the "UK lacks the US's direct influence over the AI industry, the EU's market-shaping power, and China's sheer economic weight and technological dynamism," they undersell the UK's role in AI safety. 

There are gobs of institutions and programs that research on AI policy and technical safety: [Oxford Martin AI Governance](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/ai-governance), [London AI Safety Research Labs](https://www.lasrlabs.org/), the [Institute for Law and AI's Cambridge office](https://law-ai.org/about-the-institute-for-law-ai/), and [the GovAI London office](https://www.governance.ai/). Additionally, there are the [ERA fellowships at Cambridge](https://erafellowship.org/fellowship), and [MATS fellowships have a London option](https://www.matsprogram.org/) as well.  Perhaps this is happening despite government policy, but these institutions and programs are building a dynamic community of AI experts. The UK may be better positioned on AI than it realizes.  

### The EU Confronts Old Challenges to Deal with a New Technology

The EU published its ["A Transformative AI Strategy for Europe."](https://transformative-ai.eu/) It's over 200 pages of bureaucratic speak, so there will be some skimming involved. If pressed for time, you should read [Europe's Difficult Choices on AI](https://www.ft.com/content/f054f927-b512-452a-b494-ea53f5ac1079?sharetype=blocked) by Mario Draghi. He conveys many of the same concerns as the EU document albeit in a more blunt style.

I take it back. The report is not as bureacratic sounding as I had feared. There is some hyperbole in the report -- "access to frontier AI capabilities is the largest determinant of military power" -- but they are correct to worry about the EU's relative position on developing and controlling AI. Things are moving quickly, and this seems like one of those technologies with first-mover advantage. The tone is much more urgent than the issues of data sovereignty and cloud computing because with cloud computing there is no risk of cyber/bio/chem/WMD/human annihilation. 


1. **Create a Member State Alliance for Supply Chain Security** -- Europe hosts chokepoints in global AI supply chain; EU needs to leverage that to make sure it gets access to frontier AI models.
2. **Make European institutions ready to act in a world with transformative AI** -- incorporate AI technology and expertise into institutions. The EU needs "deep frontier AI expertise that covers both technical and governance aspects." This is exactly what the UK is developing as mentioned above. 
3. **Secure Europe's share of global AI compute** -- the EU is a net importer of AI compute, so it needs to make building data centers easier. According to Draghi, it takes twice as long to build a data center in Germany as it does in the US. The report draws an analogy to Russia's export of gas to Germany as an example of leverage. 
4. **Ensure Resilience to AI crsies** -- doom prep for cyber attack, bio/chem weapons, Skynet scenarios. This is where the OAI/HF incident is mentioned. This recommendation is about emergency preparation and creating contingency plans. 
5. **Make Europe the global leader in AI assurance technology** -- become the leader in AI hardware that is safe and transparent. This recommendation is about investing in research and development.

So there are some underlying issues: the EU lost access to the frontier AI models; the EU doesn't have the organic capability to develop cutting edge AI; and it struggles to build out the needed infrastructure, such as data centers. The latter problem is both one caused by regulations and lack of funds. Draghi notes that the EU needs to pool its capital to build the infrastructure and secure access to the compute. 

On X, [Lennart Heim makes some great observations](https://twitter.com/ohlennart/status/2099941943530672263) about this strategy, which I completely agree with. He says that he would emphasize:
1. Partner with the US as the mainline strategy
2. Europe's biggest economic opportunity may be in AI complements (robotics, manufacturing, biotech). In other words, the second order effects from frontier AI.


### China

When it comes to China's AI policy, [Kyle Chan is the authority](https://www.highcapacity.org/p/chinas-global-ai-strategy), so let's go off a recent Substack post from him.

**Disclaimer**: As always, when discussing China, one must invoke the standard DC platitudes: "the characters for crisis are a combination of danger and opportunity" and "the US thinks in decades, China thinks in centuries." With that out of the way, let's dive in.

China's AI policy stems from its principal foreign policy goal of "de-centering the US from the global order and elevating China's image on the international stage." This is more of an anti-foreign policy, per Chan. To accomplish this, China is pursuing an open AI paradigm to the closed American one. China is Google Android (an open source mobile operating system) while the US is Apple iOS (a proprietary mobile operating system). By acting now, they will prevent the United States from cornering the AI market. 

There is some business logic behind this. I discussed this with Claude earlier, and the Clanka called this a classic loss-leader strategy. Draw customers in with the free offering and recoup the financial investment with the ancillary services. As Chan notes, this also earns China some goodwill and allows it to position itself as the magnanimous one. 

Something I learned is that open-source AI models don't get access to user data with which to train their models, and Chan says that Chinese AI firms are pressure to commercialize their AI services. I'm surprised the Chinese government doesn't bankroll those companies for a little while longer. 

This is the latest example of China's pattern of oscillating between control and diffusion, between security and development. They want to develop this cutting-edge technology, but they afraid of lossing control of it. In sum, Chan expects China to pivot to a hybrid model: charge for the good stuff, give away the cheap AI models.
