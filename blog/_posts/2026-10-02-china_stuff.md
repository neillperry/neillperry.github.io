### Interest in China

I am genuinely interested in China. I've read a few books on imperial and modern Chinese history, and I studied Mandarin for three years, which I promptly forgot. In the AI policy space, everyone talks about China. It's useful and important topic, but it does feel oversaturated at times. In this post, I will run through all the articles I've been meaning to read. 


### Underlying Economic Fundamentals

First, one must consider the underlying economic fundamentals. Michael Pettis has written and warned about the state of Chinese investment. [He compares the current economic situation](https://substack.com/home/post/p-209800099) in China with 1980s Japan. Both then and now, "[t]he mistake was to confuse technological leadership with the sustainability of the economic model that financed it." [In his paper for the Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/china-financial-markets/2026/04/is-chinas-high-quality-investment-output-economically-viable), he argues that state-directed investment is unsustainable because it's driven by political demands and not actual returns. 

I haven't seen anyone in China / AI policy space mention these concerns. Maybe Kyle Chan, I'll have to double check. 

### 中国人工智能政策

My biggest complaint about the AI China policy writing is how little they refer to primary sources. So [I appreciated Henry Gao calling](https://twitter.com/henrysgao/status/2098960347839521249) out [this article by Chen Yixin](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/jsc97cKVYOcHuI_WisvOxw), China's Minister of State Security (MSS), on AI.  

In that article, Chen identifies AI (人工智能) as a strategic technology that China should seek to be the "lead goose" (头雁) on. Chen then identifies the risks that AI poses, and this is interesting because it reveals CCP's view of the technology.

(I am going off a Google Translate of the text)

1. Threat to internal Chinese stability - "By waging public opinion and cognitive warfare against us, they directly threaten our political, institutional, and ideological security."
2. Cybersecurtiy - (cites Mythos and GPT-5.5-Cyber), AI lowers the barrier to entry, accelerate vulnerability discovery and exploit vectors
3. Information Leakage - he specifically calls out the risks of using OpenClaw ("龙虾") and other foreign AI products, which he considers insecure
4. AI Restrictions - he says "certain nations" (🇺🇸) use their "accumulated advantages in fundamental AI theory, model architecture, and core computing power" to limit other countries' access to those same technologies. 
5. Undermine social governance - AI could amplify social biases, and it has outgrown existing tech governance frameworks
6. Change the nature of military conflict - he mentions the role AI played in intelligence analysis and fusion in the U.S.-Iran War. 

To ameliorate those risks, he has some guidelines. First, "uphold the comprehensive leadership of the Party." This reminds me of what [Kevin Rudd wrote in The Avoidable War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Avoidable_War). Chinese leadership's principal concern is regime survival. Not surprising, but it's a subtext that I tend to forget when reading Chinese government policy. There's more recommendations about improving cybersecurity and fostering R&D, but this is all I feel like reviewing. 

Another article that [I had bookmarked was Kyle Chan's Op-Ed on China's](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/17/opinion/ai-china-america-risk.html) concerns about A.I. A lot of what he reports can also be found in Chen's article. "For the moment, Chinese policymakers are more worried about the political and social risks caused by A.I [than they are about existential risks]." 

### How China Thinks About AI

Derek Thompson has a [decent Substack post about and titled this](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/how-china-sees-ai). He pulls in a variety of experts and asks questions. 

"China regards AI almost polytheistically, as one of many important tech bets, including cars and energy; America treats AI more monotheistically, as the centerpiece of its grand tech strategy." This is a subheading in the article, so I don't know whether it's Derek Thompson or one of his experts. It is interesting to recall just how much money China has poured into car manufacturing and solar panels. [According to Reuters, they invested $920 billion](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/chinas-clean-energy-investments-nearing-scale-global-fossil-investments-2025-02-19/) in clean energy just in 2024. A Google search says that they've invested $230 billion in electric vehicles, but I can't find that source. 

[Zilan Qian argues in her post that China](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/how-china-hopes-to-build-agi-through) is approaching AGI from the realm of robotics, an area in which it excels, while Americans conceive of AGI as a software-driven achievement. 

Matt Sheehan is skeptical of the claim that if we lose to China in the AI race, then it's all over. The dynamic is a more multi-dimensional than that. Besides, how are they or anyone else going to control super intelligence? 

Jordan Schneider notes that AI's ability to augment internal surveillance and domestic monitoring. 

Paul Triolo observes that there is an actual domestic Chinese AI safety community.
